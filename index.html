Job Market Paper

Distributional Consequences of Police Allocation: Evidence from Chicago

Best Student Paper (Honorable Mention) at the 2026 Urban Economics Association North American Meeting

Abstract: Municipal police deployment involves a fundamental location tradeoff: more patrol in one place means less elsewhere. I study the distributional consequences in Chicago using granular data, quasi-experimental variation, and a quantitative urban model. The observed allocation is consistent with a dual objective of crime minimization and land value maximization, generating disproportionate patrol in high-income neighborhoods. Shifting toward pure crime minimization reduces citywide crime but hurts every resident type. In neighborhoods gaining police, rising rents erode welfare gains for residents with the lowest willingness to pay for crime reduction; in neighborhoods losing police, higher crime compounds losses for those with the highest willingness to pay. A modest reallocation toward neighborhoods with high marginal valuation of crime reduction improves welfare for all types. Shifting the citywide police level creates limited welfare gains, pointing to reallocation as the main policy lever.

Public Transit Expansion and Crime: Evidence from Chicago New draft coming soon!

[Abstract]

Public transit expansions often face resistance from residents concerned about the potential spread of crime into their neighborhoods. To assess these concerns, I leverage a granular dataset from the Chicago Police Department that links crime incidents to offenders’ home addresses. Using variation in travel costs induced by a train shutdown, I document a short-run decline in downtown property crime committed by offenders affected by the shutdown. To study the distributional impacts, I develop a spatial equilibrium model of residential and workplace location choices that features interactions between offenders and workers. In a counterfactual train line expansion, I find that while offenders relocate near the extended line, the resulting increase in property crime is localized and propagates only minimally to other parts of the city.

The Contribution of High-Skilled Immigrants to Innovation in the United States

With Shai Bernstein, Rebecca Diamond, Tim McQuade, and Beatriz Pousada

Forthcoming at American Economic Review

[Abstract]

We characterize the contribution of immigrants to US innovation. Leveraging new data, we use age of SSN assignment to identify immigrant status. Immigrants represent 16 percent of inventors, but authored 23 percent of patents. Immigrant inventors contribute to knowledge diffusion across borders. They disproportionately rely on foreign technologies and inventor collaborations. Using variation from premature inventor deaths, we find immigrant inventors create stronger innovation productivity spillovers on their collaborators, as compared to US-born inventors. A simple model implies immigrants are responsible for 32 percent of aggregate innovation, over half of which is due to human capital externalities on US-born collaborators.
